# Prove precision first, then close gaps

As of late November 2026, AI pull-request review is a crowded, fast-moving market where the same core features (inline comments with one-click suggestions, severity labels, a PR summary, repo rules files such as AGENTS.md, and some form of learning from feedback) are now table stakes, while vendors compete on whole-repo context, multi-agent verification, autofix agents and usage-based pricing. Quality cannot be compared across vendors with any confidence: **every vendor that publishes a benchmark wins its own**, rankings flip between benchmarks because ground truth and metrics differ, and the most rigorous public yardstick, GitHub's ReviewBench (October 2026), was built by a vendor ([GitHub blog](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/reviewbench-an-open-benchmark-for-ai-code-review/); [DeepSource](https://deepsource.com/blog/ai-code-review-benchmarks)). For our open-source, self-hostable reviewer, the most important gaps are cheap ones: committable suggestion blocks, reading AGENTS.md/CLAUDE.md/REVIEW.md, a walkthrough, severity thresholds with comment caps, auto-resolving fixed threads, and an agent hand-off prompt. The expensive ones (cross-file context, a verification pass, PR commands, GitLab) belong in the next quarter. Our strongest position combines four things almost no competitor offers together: free self-hosting with bring-your-own model or subscription, a locked-down deterministic sandbox with honest per-layer status, a review that a PR cannot weaken, and an **open benchmark that publishes our own precision and recall**. No vendor publishes a false-positive rate, so building the benchmark first is both the quality control for every later feature and the most credible thing we can market.

## Benchmarks disagree because they measure different things

A single tool can look like a leader or an also-ran depending on who scored it. Greptile's July 2025 benchmark (50 real bugs across Sentry, Cal.com, Grafana, Keycloak and Discourse) reported **Greptile 82%, Bugbot 58%, Copilot 54%, CodeRabbit 44%, Graphite 6%**. It measured recall only: no false positives were scored, and its own case tables put Copilot at 26/50 (52%) rather than the 54% headline ([Greptile benchmarks](https://www.greptile.com/benchmarks.md)). When Augment re-scored the same PRs with corrected golden comments and precision included, Greptile fell to **45% F-score**, behind Augment (59%) and Bugbot (49%). That run had its own conflict of interest: Augment edited the gold set after seeing tool outputs and did not disclose its matching judge ([Augment blog](https://www.augmentcode.com/blog/we-benchmarked-7-ai-code-review-tools-on-real-world-prs-here-are-the-results); [Qodo glossary](https://www.qodo.ai/glossary/what-is-ai-code-review-benchmarks/)). On Martian's MIT-licensed Code Review Bench, Greptile, CodeRabbit, Kilo and Baz each claim #1 on a different view or snapshot ([Kilo](https://blog.kilo.ai/p/martians-independent-benchmark-tested); [Baz](https://baz.co/resources/baz-1-in-precision-in-independent-code-review-bench)). DeepSource put it bluntly: "Every AI code review vendor benchmarks itself, and wins" ([DeepSource](https://deepsource.com/blog/ai-code-review-benchmarks)).

The academic results set a sober baseline. **Frontier systems detect only about 15–33% of known defects** on SWE-PRBench and CR-Bench-v2. SWE-PRBench also found that every model it tested got worse as context grew from diff-only to full AST, import-graph and test context, and that a structured 2k-token diff-plus-summary prompt beat 2.5k tokens of raw context ([arXiv 2603.26130](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26130); [arXiv 2611.02566](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02566)). That directly challenges the "whole-repo index" marketing every leader leans on, and argues for adding context only where a measured ablation shows it helps. The field has dropped BLEU-style text similarity. CodeReviewer-era references turned out to be noisy, and the current task taxonomy has three levels: defect detection (recall), comment validity and usefulness (precision), and comment text quality ([arXiv 2203.09095](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.09095); [CRScore](https://aclanthology.org/2025.naacl-long.457)).

In production, the dominant metric is whether a comment led to a code change before merge. Vendors call it addressed, resolved, accepted or acted-on rate. The best peer-reviewed anchor is Atlassian's year-long RovoDev deployment: **38.70% of AI comments were resolved versus 44.45% of human comments**, across more than 1,900 repos, while median PR cycle time fell 30.8% ([arXiv 2601.01129](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.01129v1)). Cursor's 78% resolution rate for Bugbot suggests a far more selective commenting policy. Because a tool that says less scores higher on this metric, any addressed rate must be reported next to comment volume ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/bugbot-learning)). Another industrial study is a useful warning: **73.8% of automated comments were resolved, yet average PR closure time rose from 5h52m to 8h20m** ([arXiv 2412.18531](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.18531v2)). High resolution rates do not guarantee faster delivery.

### Differentiators we can credibly own

The differentiators come from things competitors structurally cannot or will not do, not from feature count.

**An open, reproducible quality benchmark that includes precision.** No vendor publishes a false-positive rate, every vendor-run benchmark crowns its sponsor, and DeepSource's prescription (real ground truth, versioned public data, a judge blinded to tool identity, at least 100 items, raw outputs published) has no community-run implementation ([DeepSource](https://deepsource.com/blog/ai-code-review-benchmarks)). Publishing per-PR outputs, judge prompts and cost per PR for each release, including bad numbers, makes our honesty claim checkable. It also extends the "honest per-layer status" principle we already apply to deterministic checks to the AI layer.

**Deterministic evidence in a locked-down sandbox, fed to the model.** The pure-LLM reviewers explicitly do not run tools. Claude Code Review "works from reading the diff and the surrounding code, without building or running your code," Codex tells users to "leave formatting and lint checks to CI," and Graphite, Greptile and Bugbot document no linters ([Claude docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/code-review); [Codex docs](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/third-party/github); [Graphite](https://graphite.com/docs/ai-reviews.md)). Only CodeRabbit bundles a large tool catalog, and only inside a closed SaaS. We already run the repo's own tsc and ESLint in a secret-less container with honest PASSED/SKIPPED/ERROR status. The next steps are to pass those results into the AI call as evidence, so the model triages and explains real compiler and linter errors instead of guessing; to add a few high-signal tools (Semgrep/Opengrep, Gitleaks, actionlint/zizmor for workflow changes); and eventually to run the repo's tests in the sandbox. That would put us between the LLM-native and AppSec-bundled camps, a gap both the challenger and platform research identified independently.

**Free self-hosting with bring-your-own model or subscription, and visible cost.** Self-hosting with BYO keys is enterprise-only at CodeRabbit, Greptile, Qodo, Sourcery, DeepSource and Sonar. Claude Code Review cannot serve ZDR or HIPAA organizations at all, and per-review prices run from about $0.25 (Copilot Lite) through $1–10 (Greptile, Bugbot) to roughly $15–25 (Claude) ([CodeRabbit pricing](https://www.coderabbit.ai/pricing); [DeepSource pricing](https://deepsource.com/pricing); [Claude docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/code-review)). Using a developer's existing Claude Code or Codex subscription has no competitor equivalent. An OpenAI-compatible endpoint also covers local models for air-gapped use, which Sonar sells only on Server Enterprise. Our existing token and USD reporting with ceilings answers the Bugbot cost backlash directly. We should put "cost per review" in the summary footer and in benchmark reports.

**A review that the PR under review cannot weaken.** We read the repo allow-list at the base commit, let repos only tighten the gate, redact secrets before the AI call, and handle prompt injection. That matches the hardening Claude applies to REVIEW.md, which it reads only from the protected base branch ([Claude customize](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/code-review-customize)), but goes further. By contrast, Copilot reads instructions from the PR's **head** branch ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/concepts/agents/code-review)). When we add the rules files from gap 2, we must keep base-commit reading, and we should state this guarantee publicly.

**Transparent, version-controlled learning.** Greptile's inferred rules may not be viewable, and Graphite does not document its learning mechanism ([Greptile](https://www.greptile.com/docs/how-greptile-works/memory-and-learning.md); [Graphite](https://graphite.com/docs/ai-reviews.md)). Two patterns fit self-hosting well. One is CodeRabbit's `emit path instructions`, which opens a PR with suggested config ([CodeRabbit commands](https://docs.coderabbit.ai/reference/review-commands.md)). The other is Graphite's per-rule acceptance dashboard ([Graphite customization](https://graphite.com/docs/ai-review-customization)). Combined, they give us this: mine dismissals, 👎 reactions and addressed findings locally, propose rule changes as a PR to the repo's rule file, and show per-rule addressed and dismissed rates in `stats` and the Web App. Every learned behavior then has a diff and an owner.

**Gates without approving.** The AI-native reviewers are advisory: Claude's check never fails, and Codex rules "don't replace" branch protection. The free Sonar Community Build reportedly cannot decorate PRs at all ([Claude docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/code-review); [dev.to](https://dev.to/rahulxsingh/sonarqube-vs-sonarcloud-self-hosted-vs-cloud-code-quality-2026-dkj)). A free, self-hosted commit status that fails on gating findings or deterministic failures in new code, and that branch protection can require, is uncommon.

There is also a timing opportunity. PR-Agent, the only free self-hostable incumbent, has been handed off by its creator. Amazon Q for GitHub reaches end of support on 30 January 2027 with no documented automated reviewer to replace it ([AWS docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/amazonq/latest/qdeveloper-ug/amazon-q-for-github.html)). Both are migration windows. Reading PR-Agent's `.pr_agent.toml` intent and the other vendors' rules files makes switching cheap.

### Features to skip on purpose

We should decline features that conflict with our principles, cost more than they return, or belong to a different product. **Auto-approval** (Greptile on a 5/5 score, CodeRabbit `auto_approvals`, Copilot approvals counting toward required reviews, Sourcery's low-risk auto-approve) contradicts "never approves". A required commit status gives teams the same "merge when clean" outcome without handing approval authority to a model ([Greptile llms.txt](https://www.greptile.com/docs/llms.txt); [GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/concepts/agents/code-review)). **Autofix agents that commit to the PR branch** (CodeRabbit `autofix`/`fix-ci`, Bugbot Autofix, Copilot's cloud-agent hand-off, Kiro `/kiro all`) need write credentials inside the job, which breaks our secret-less sandbox and adds cost. Suggestion blocks plus an agent hand-off prompt and a local `fix` command deliver most of the value. **Poems and fun extras** are losing favor: CodeRabbit now turns its poem off by default ([CodeRabbit config](https://docs.coderabbit.ai/reference/configuration.md)). **Test and docstring generation** has largely left PR-review products. No active product documents a PR-level `/test` command, and CodeRabbit's current reference no longer lists docstring generation ([CodeRabbit commands](https://docs.coderabbit.ai/reference/review-commands.md)).

Four more features are heavy infrastructure that does not fit a lightweight self-hosted tool. A **vector-embedding codebase index** is expensive to host and unproven by independent benchmarks; read-only agentic retrieval in the sandbox is cheaper to test. **Runtime and E2E verification** (CodeRabbit Verify, Greptile TREX) is the frontier but heavy, and should wait until running unit tests in the sandbox proves its value. **Stacked PRs and merge queues** are Graphite's product, not a reviewer feature. **AppSec-suite expansion** (CodeAnt's CSPM, DAST and pentest SKUs) is a different market. We should also defer an **IDE extension** and **long-tail hosts** (Gerrit, CodeCommit, Perforce): a CLI agent mode plus MCP reaches IDE agents more cheaply, and PR-Agent already serves the long tail. Finally, we should not chase CodeRabbit's 59-tool breadth. A few tools with honest status and LLM triage beat many noisy ones.

## A benchmark we can run ourselves in two weeks

The plan copies ReviewBench's design, which is the most complete public methodology: multi-source gold validated by a rubric, grounded metrics as the headline, a published and human-validated judge, three runs per PR, and an offline-to-online check ([GitHub blog](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/reviewbench-an-open-benchmark-for-ai-code-review/)). It adds safeguards against the contamination and post-hoc gold edits that undermined the vendor benchmarks.

**Datasets, in four tiers.** The *comparability tier* is the public 50-PR Greptile/Augment set (the `ai-code-review-evaluations` GitHub org). Running it puts our numbers next to Augment's published P/R/F table for Bugbot, Codex, Claude Code, CodeRabbit and Copilot. It is old, public and probably tuned on, so it is for comparison only and never a held-out test ([Augment blog](https://www.augmentcode.com/blog/we-benchmarked-7-ai-code-review-tools-on-real-world-prs-here-are-the-results)). The *open academic tier* has three parts. CR-Bench-Verified (174 instances) gives known-defect detection and usefulness rates. SWE-PRBench (350 PRs, with three frozen context configurations and a judge validated at κ = 0.75) is the right harness for ablating any context feature before we ship it. Qodo's PR-Review-Bench on Hugging Face (100 PRs, 580 issues, rules supplied through AGENTS.md) tests whether our rules-file reading works ([arXiv 2611.02566](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02566); [arXiv 2603.26130](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26130); [Qodo benchmark](https://qodo.ai/ai-code-review-benchmark/)). The *private held-out tier* is 50–100 bug-introducing PRs **merged after our models' training cutoffs**, weighted toward TypeScript, Angular and React repos where our deterministic checks apply. We build it with Greptile's trace-back method (find a bug-fix PR, blame back to the commit that introduced the bug, re-open it as a PR on a clean fork) and Qodo's filters (3+ files, 50–15,000 changed lines, no revert) ([Greptile](https://www.greptile.com/benchmarks.md); [Qodo blog](https://www.qodo.ai/blog/how-we-built-a-real-world-benchmark-for-ai-code-review/)). Two humans label severity and category and record their agreement. The set is frozen and versioned, with a separate dev split for tuning. The fourth tier is a *deterministic regression set* that we construct ourselves: PRs with seeded tsc errors, ESLint violations, known-vulnerable dependency versions and fake secrets. These checks should reach near-100% recall, and their per-layer status should never silently read SKIPPED. Finally, we should submit to **ReviewBench**, the only public leaderboard with a validated judge. Its 25-PR then 219-PR ×3 container flow fits a BYO-key tool, but it is not confirmed whether it accepts non-commercial OSS entrants.

**Matching rule.** A gold issue counts as caught only if an **inline** comment (summary mentions don't count, following Greptile's rule) is in the same file, within ±5 lines or the same hunk, **and** an LLM judge using a published rubric says it describes the same root cause. Line overlap alone misses findings anchored at a different line, and a judge alone can hallucinate matches. This hybrid rule is our recommendation, not a published result. The judge must come from a different model family than the reviewer, must be blind to the tool's identity, and must be validated on about 100 hand-labelled (comment, gold) pairs, targeting κ ≥ 0.7 ([DeepSource](https://deepsource.com/blog/ai-code-review-benchmarks); [arXiv 2603.26130](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26130)). Unmatched comments go to a judge-plus-human "valid extra" bucket that feeds augmented precision. Following ReviewBench, augmented precision is a per-system diagnostic only and is never used to rank tools.

**Offline metrics.** The headline numbers are grounded precision, recall and F1 (F2 if we decide recall matters more), reported as mean ± standard deviation over three runs. Supporting metrics are per-severity recall, comments per PR, the share of PRs with zero comments, tokens and USD per PR, p50/p95 latency, and the deterministic-layer status distribution. Every change to a model, prompt, context strategy or verification pass runs as an **ablation** against the frozen held-out set: diff-only versus diff plus changed files versus added retrieval, single pass versus verify pass, and with versus without tool results in the prompt. Differences inside run-to-run variance are ties; SWE-PRBench found its top four models statistically indistinguishable ([arXiv 2603.26130](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26130)).

**Head-to-head comparisons.** Because we cannot audit vendor numbers, we should run competitors ourselves where we can. PR-Agent is MIT-licensed and self-hostable, so it runs in our harness on identical inputs. Claude Code's local `/code-review` and Codex CLI `/review` run on the same subscriptions our users already have. Several SaaS products are free on public or OSS repos (CodeRabbit, Sourcery, DeepSource, Codacy, Greptile by application), so mirroring the held-out PRs into public forks lets us collect their default-settings output for the same scoring ([CodeRabbit pricing](https://www.coderabbit.ai/pricing); [Sourcery pricing](https://www.sourcery.ai/pricing); [DeepSource pricing](https://deepsource.com/pricing); [Codacy pricing](https://www.codacy.com/pricing)). This approach is feasible but untested here. Vendor terms and rate limits need checking, and a public fork makes the held-out PRs public, so this run should use a dedicated comparison split that we then retire.

**Online metrics.** Extend `stats` and the Web App beyond posted, resolved and dismissed counts. Add an **addressed rate**: an LLM judges whether the code at merge fixes each finding, the same method Cursor and Copilot use ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/building-bugbot); [GitHub blog](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/reviewbench-an-open-benchmark-for-ai-code-review/)). Add the suggestion-commit rate (once gap 1 ships), 👎/dismiss rate per rule and per category, comments per PR, time to first review, and cost per PR. Always show addressed rate next to volume. The external reference points are Atlassian's 38.70% AI and 44.45% human resolution rates and Cursor's cross-vendor table (Bugbot 78%, Copilot 47%, Gemini 31%), each labelled as a proxy measured by someone else ([arXiv 2601.01129](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.01129v1); [Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/bugbot-learning)).

**Reporting.** Each release publishes a versioned results file and a static page. It lists dataset versions, model and provider, prompt hash, judge model and prompt, judge κ, per-PR raw outputs and verdicts, P/R/F1 with variance, cost and latency, and a "what this does not prove" section, following Qodo's checklist ([Qodo glossary](https://www.qodo.ai/glossary/what-is-ai-code-review-benchmarks/)). Results are never merged across metrics: catch rate, F1 and resolution rate stay in separate tables.

## A phased roadmap ranked by impact over effort

The sequencing rule is that **the benchmark ships first**. That way every later precision or context feature launches with a measured delta and does not just add cost.

The two-to-four-week batch is mostly low effort because it builds on machinery we already have: structured findings, markers, base-commit config reading and the stats store. The quarter's items are where the real differentiation sits. Each one has a go/no-go criterion from the benchmark, which keeps us from repeating the vendor pattern of shipping context and agents on faith.

## Conclusion

The market has inverted what a newcomer should compete on. Features that looked like differentiators in 2025 (summaries, inline suggestions, rules files, noise control, even learning) have become commodities that we can match in weeks. What no competitor offers is **verifiable** quality: no vendor publishes a false-positive rate, benchmark leadership is mostly a function of who built the benchmark, and the one result that replicates across independent academic work is uncomfortable for everyone, since frontier reviewers catch roughly a sixth to a third of known defects and more raw context makes them worse. That makes an open benchmark more than a marketing asset for us. It is the control system that decides whether verification passes, cross-file context and learned rules earn their cost.

The structural opening is equally clear. Self-hosting with your own model is either an enterprise SKU or unavailable from every commercial vendor. The only free incumbent has been spun out by its creator. AWS is leaving the space. Usage-based pricing has made per-review cost a visible pain point. A reviewer that runs real tools in a locked-down sandbox, cannot be weakened by the PR it reviews, shows its cost on every review, and publishes its precision and recall with raw outputs offers something the SaaS leaders cannot easily copy, because doing so would expose their own numbers.
